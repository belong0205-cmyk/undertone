# Plan and cost recipes — SITHKOP010; Receive, store and maintain stock — SITXINV006 (also SITXINV007 Purchase goods)

## Standard recipes
- A standard recipe is the tested, documented recipe used every time: name, yield (number of portions), portion size, ingredients with exact quantities and units, method step by step, cooking times/temperatures, equipment, presentation/plating instructions and photo, garnish, storage and shelf life, allergens/dietary information, cost per portion and selling price.
- Benefits: consistent quality, taste and presentation; portion control; accurate costing and pricing; reduced waste; easier ordering; training new staff; meets customer expectations and allergen requirements.
- Developing a recipe: research/adapt, test and trial, evaluate (taste, appearance, cost, time, equipment, customer feedback), adjust, document, cost, train staff.

## Key formulas
- Yield % = edible portion (EP) weight ÷ as-purchased (AP) weight × 100.
  Example: 10 kg whole fish → 4.5 kg fillets → yield 45%.
- Real (EP) cost per kg = AP price per kg ÷ yield as a decimal. Example: fish $18/kg AP at 45% yield → $18 ÷ 0.45 = $40/kg of fillet.
- Quantity to order (AP) = EP needed ÷ yield. Example: need 3 kg trimmed beef at 80% yield → 3 ÷ 0.8 = 3.75 kg.
- Recipe cost = sum of (quantity used × unit cost) for every ingredient (include oil, seasoning, garnish — often a "sundries/Q" allowance of 2–5%).
- Portion cost = total recipe cost ÷ number of portions.
- Food cost % = portion cost ÷ selling price (without GST) × 100. It shows how many cents of every sales dollar go on food. I compare it with the target, usually 25–35%. It helps me set prices, spot waste or big portions and protect gross profit.
- Selling price (ex-GST) = portion cost ÷ target food cost % (as a decimal). Example: cost $6.00, target 30% → $6 ÷ 0.30 = $20.00 ex-GST → menu price incl. GST $20 × 1.1 = $22.00.
- Mark-up method: selling price = cost × mark-up factor (e.g. 3 to 3.5 times cost) — food cost % = 100 ÷ factor (factor 3 ≈ 33%).
- Gross profit (GP) = selling price (ex-GST) − food cost; GP % = GP ÷ selling price × 100 (GP% + food cost % = 100%).
- Removing GST from a price that includes it: divide by 1.1 (GST component = price ÷ 11).
- Conversion (scaling) factor = new yield ÷ old yield. Example: recipe for 10 portions, need 25 → factor 2.5; multiply every ingredient by 2.5. Spices, salt, gelatine, raising agents and cooking times don't always scale proportionally — test and adjust.
- Unit conversions: 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 L = 1000 mL, 1 L of water = 1 kg; tablespoon (Australian) = 20 mL, teaspoon = 5 mL, cup = 250 mL.
- Unit cost: purchase price ÷ purchase quantity (e.g. $12.50 for a 5 kg bag of flour = $2.50/kg = $0.0025/g).

## Pricing considerations
Food cost and labour cost, overheads (rent, energy, equipment), target profit, competitor prices, customer market and perceived value, type of establishment and menu, portion size, seasonal availability of ingredients, popularity (menu engineering: stars = popular & profitable, ploughhorses = popular & low profit, puzzles = profitable & unpopular, dogs = neither).
Typical food cost targets 25–35% (fine dining may be lower; cafés/pubs vary).
- Seasonal ingredients: in season they are cheaper and better in flavour and quality. They are easy to get and often local. Out of season they are dear, poorer and imported. I plan menus around the seasons. I use "market price" or a changing specials board.
- Customer preference: the menu must sell. I know the target market: age, culture, budget, trends, dietary needs. Unpopular dishes waste stock and labour. I use sales data and feedback.
- Dietary requirements: I plan vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, dairy-free and allergen-safe options. I record allergens on the recipe card. Substitutes may cost more and separate prep adds time, so I cost it in.
- Cultural and ethnic influences: customers' and local backgrounds shape ingredients, flavours and methods. Examples: halal, kosher, Asian, Mediterranean, Indigenous. I use authentic ingredients and techniques. I respect religious food rules and describe the dish honestly.
- Feedback: customers, floor staff, sales figures and plate waste show what to adjust. Taste, portion, price, look. I test the change, update the standard recipe and keep what works.
- Supplier price rise (e.g. seafood): re-cost the recipe; raise the price, trim the portion slightly, use a cheaper species or garnish, make it a market-price special, or negotiate with suppliers.
- Costing a chicken pasta dish: list every ingredient and quantity, unit cost from invoices (after yield), quantity × unit cost, add 2–5% sundries, total ÷ portions = portion cost; e.g. $4.50 at a 30% target → $15.00 ex-GST → $16.50 with GST.
- Popular but low profit (ploughhorse): re-cost, slightly smaller portion or cheaper side, small price rise, pair with high-margin sides or drinks, keep quality.
- Degustation portions too big and waste high: smaller tasting portions, lighter courses between rich ones, standard recipes with weighed portions, trial and get feedback, track plate waste.

## Menu planning principles
Balance of colour, texture, flavour, cooking methods and ingredients across the menu (avoid repetition — e.g. not two cream sauces or two pastry dishes); seasonality and local produce; nutritional balance and dietary options (V, VG, GF); target market and price point; kitchen equipment and staff skill; storage capacity; cost and profitability; menu descriptions must be accurate (Australian Consumer Law — no misleading claims e.g. "fresh" or "local").
- À la carte: dishes are listed and priced one by one and cooked to order. There is more choice and exact portion costing, but more labour and waste risk. Buffet: one fixed price, and guests serve themselves from a display. Bulk production means less labour per cover, but portion control is lost. So I cost from the average eaten per head. I watch hot-holding ≥60°C, cold ≤5°C and the 2-hour/4-hour rule. Also sneeze guards and a utensil per dish.

## Receiving stock (SITXINV006)
1. I check the delivery against the purchase order and invoice. Product, quantity, weight, brand, price. I check quality. Probe temperature between packs: PHF ≤5°C or ≥60°C, frozen food frozen (3.2.2 cl 5). Packaging intact: no dents, swelling, leaks or broken vacuum seals. Use-by dates, no pests, fresh smell and colour, a clean refrigerated vehicle.
2. I reject or record non-conforming items and tell the supplier. I ask for a credit note and note it on the docket. I tell the supervisor. I sign only when satisfied. I record temperatures (Std 3.2.2A). I store at once: frozen first, then chilled, then dry goods.

## Storing stock
- Dry store: cool (about 10–21°C, ideally below 15°C), dry, ventilated, pest-proof, out of sunlight. Goods at least 15 cm off the floor. Opened goods decanted into labelled covered food-grade containers. Chemicals stored separately.
- Cool room ≤5°C (ideally 1–4°C): raw meat, poultry and seafood on the bottom shelves. Cooked/RTE food above; covered, labelled, door closed. Freezer ≤−18°C: wrapped tightly, labelled, dated.
- Labels: product, date received/prepared, use-by, batch, initials, allergens. Rotation: FIFO (new stock behind old) or FEFO (first expired, first out). Record fridge/freezer temperatures at least daily; report faults.

## Maintaining stock
- Par level: the minimum stock I need between deliveries. Order quantity = par level − stock on hand, plus expected usage before the delivery.
- Stocktake: I count the stock at regular intervals (weekly or monthly). It shows usage, cost of goods sold, losses, waste, theft and slow movers. Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − closing stock.
- Documents: purchase orders, delivery dockets, invoices, credit notes, bin cards, inventory software, wastage sheets. Record and dispose of spoiled or expired stock; investigate causes; lock stores.
- Overstocking: spoilage, cash and space tied up, theft. Understocking: 86'd items, unhappy customers, costly emergency buys.

## Purchasing (SITXINV007)
Approved suppliers (quality, price, reliability, food safety certification). Specifications (product, grade, size, temperature), quotes, ethical and sustainable buying. Check invoices against prices.
