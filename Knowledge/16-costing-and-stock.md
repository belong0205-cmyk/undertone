# Plan and cost recipes — SITHKOP010; Receive, store and maintain stock — SITXINV006 (also SITXINV007 Purchase goods)

## Standard recipes
- A standard recipe is the tested, documented recipe used every time: name, yield (number of portions), portion size, ingredients with exact quantities and units, method step by step, cooking times/temperatures, equipment, presentation/plating instructions and photo, garnish, storage and shelf life, allergens/dietary information, cost per portion and selling price.
- Benefits: consistent quality, taste and presentation; portion control; accurate costing and pricing; reduced waste; easier ordering; training new staff; meets customer expectations and allergen requirements.
- Developing a recipe: research/adapt, test and trial, evaluate (taste, appearance, cost, time, equipment, customer feedback), adjust, document, cost, train staff.

## Key formulas
- Yield % = edible portion (EP) weight ÷ as-purchased (AP) weight × 100.
  Example: 10 kg whole fish → 4.5 kg fillets → yield 45%.
- Real (EP) cost per kg = AP price per kg ÷ yield as a decimal. Example: fish $18/kg AP at 45% yield → $18 ÷ 0.45 = $40/kg of fillet.
- Quantity to order (AP) = EP needed ÷ yield. Example: need 3 kg trimmed beef at 80% yield → 3 ÷ 0.8 = 3.75 kg.
- Recipe cost = sum of (quantity used × unit cost) for every ingredient (include oil, seasoning, garnish — often a "sundries/Q" allowance of 2–5%).
- Portion cost = total recipe cost ÷ number of portions.
- Food cost % = portion cost ÷ selling price (excluding GST) × 100.
- Selling price (ex-GST) = portion cost ÷ target food cost % (as a decimal). Example: cost $6.00, target 30% → $6 ÷ 0.30 = $20.00 ex-GST → menu price incl. GST $20 × 1.1 = $22.00.
- Mark-up method: selling price = cost × mark-up factor (e.g. 3 to 3.5 times cost) — food cost % = 100 ÷ factor (factor 3 ≈ 33%).
- Gross profit (GP) = selling price (ex-GST) − food cost; GP % = GP ÷ selling price × 100 (GP% + food cost % = 100%).
- Removing GST from a price that includes it: divide by 1.1 (GST component = price ÷ 11).
- Conversion (scaling) factor = new yield ÷ old yield. Example: recipe for 10 portions, need 25 → factor 2.5; multiply every ingredient by 2.5. Spices, salt, gelatine, raising agents and cooking times don't always scale proportionally — test and adjust.
- Unit conversions: 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 L = 1000 mL, 1 L of water = 1 kg; tablespoon (Australian) = 20 mL, teaspoon = 5 mL, cup = 250 mL.
- Unit cost: purchase price ÷ purchase quantity (e.g. $12.50 for a 5 kg bag of flour = $2.50/kg = $0.0025/g).

## Pricing considerations
Food cost and labour cost, overheads (rent, energy, equipment), target profit, competitor prices, customer market and perceived value, type of establishment and menu, portion size, seasonal availability of ingredients, popularity (menu engineering: stars = popular & profitable, ploughhorses = popular & low profit, puzzles = profitable & unpopular, dogs = neither).
Typical food cost targets 25–35% (fine dining may be lower; cafés/pubs vary).

## Menu planning principles
Balance of colour, texture, flavour, cooking methods and ingredients across the menu (avoid repetition — e.g. not two cream sauces or two pastry dishes); seasonality and local produce; nutritional balance and dietary options (V, VG, GF); target market and price point; kitchen equipment and staff skill; storage capacity; cost and profitability; menu descriptions must be accurate (Australian Consumer Law — no misleading claims e.g. "fresh" or "local").

## Receiving stock (SITXINV006)
1. Check delivery against the purchase order and delivery docket/invoice: product, quantity, size/weight, brand, price.
2. Check quality: temperature (chilled ≤5°C, frozen hard ≤ −15°C, hot ≥60°C) using a probe (between packs, not piercing them), use-by/best-before dates, packaging intact (no dents, swelling, tears, leaks, broken vacuum seals), signs of pests, freshness (smell, colour, texture), correct labelling, delivery vehicle clean and refrigerated.
3. Reject or record non-conforming items (wrong, damaged, out of temperature, short-dated) — notify the supplier, request a credit note, record on the docket and in the goods-received log, inform the supervisor.
4. Sign the delivery docket only when satisfied; record temperatures (Std 3.2.2A record).
5. Store immediately — frozen first, then chilled, then dry goods.

## Storing stock
- Dry store: cool (approx. 10–21°C, ideally below ~15°C), dry, well ventilated, pest-proof, out of direct sunlight; goods on shelves at least 15 cm off the floor; opened goods decanted into labelled, covered food-grade containers; chemicals stored separately.
- Cool room/refrigerator ≤5°C (ideally 1–4°C): raw meat, poultry and seafood on the bottom shelves; cooked/RTE food above; dairy, desserts; covered and labelled; air circulation (don't overload); door closed.
- Freezer −18°C or below: wrapped tightly to prevent freezer burn, labelled, dated, FIFO.
- Labels: product name, date received/prepared, use-by date, batch, initials, allergens.
- Stock rotation: FIFO (first in, first out — new stock behind older stock) or FEFO (first expired, first out).
- Monitor and record fridge/freezer temperatures (at least daily); report faults.
- Storage of specific items: fruit and vegetables — most refrigerated, but potatoes, onions, pumpkin, garlic, bananas and tomatoes in a cool dry area; eggs refrigerated; bread at room temperature or frozen.

## Maintaining stock
- Par level (par stock): the minimum quantity required between deliveries; order enough to bring stock back up to par. Order quantity = par level − stock on hand (+ expected usage before delivery, if applicable).
- Reorder point / minimum stock levels; maximum levels to avoid overstock.
- Stocktake: physical count of stock at regular intervals (weekly/monthly) to calculate usage, cost of goods sold, identify losses/waste/theft and slow-moving items.
- Food cost for a period (cost of goods sold) = opening stock + purchases − closing stock (− staff meals/credits).
- Stock control documents: purchase orders, requisitions (from store to kitchen), delivery dockets, invoices, credit notes, stock cards/bin cards, inventory software, wastage sheets, transfer notes.
- Report and record waste, spoiled, damaged, expired or out-of-date stock; dispose of it correctly; investigate causes.
- Security: lock stores, limit access, monitor deliveries and stock movement.
- Problems of overstocking: spoilage, cash tied up, storage space, theft. Under-stocking: running out (86'd items), unhappy customers, emergency purchases at higher prices.

## Purchasing (SITXINV007)
Approved suppliers (quality, price, reliability, delivery times, food safety certification, credit terms), quotes and tenders, specifications (product, grade, size, packaging, temperature), ordering by phone/online/standing orders, ethical and sustainable purchasing, checking prices against invoices.
