# Poultry and meat — SITHCCC035 Prepare poultry dishes, SITHCCC036 Prepare meat dishes

## Poultry
### Types
- Chicken: spatchcock/poussin (very young, about 400–500 g), broiler/roaster (most common, 1.2–2 kg), boiler/stewing hen (older, tougher, for stocks and braising), capon (castrated male, rich meat), free-range, organic, corn-fed.
- Duck (fatty, rich, dark meat — Peking, Muscovy), goose, turkey, quail, squab/pigeon, guinea fowl; game birds: pheasant, partridge, emu (lean red meat).
- White meat (breast — less used muscle, cooks quickly, dries out easily) vs dark meat (legs/thigh — more connective tissue and fat, suits slower cooking).
### Quality and storage
- Fresh poultry: plump breast, moist skin with no tears, bruises or dark patches, pleasant smell (no sliminess, ammonia/sour smell), cold to touch (≤5°C on delivery).
- Store raw poultry on the bottom shelf in covered trays, ≤5°C (ideally 0–3°C), use within 2 days; frozen at −18°C; thaw in the fridge (a whole bird can take 24–48 hours).
- High risk of Salmonella and Campylobacter: separate (yellow) boards, wash hands, sanitise, never wash raw chicken in the sink (splashes bacteria).
- Doneness checks: I probe the thickest part of the thigh, not touching bone, for 75°C. The juices run clear with no pink. The leg moves freely. The flesh is firm and opaque. Duck breast is often served pink; I follow workplace policy. Duck legs are cooked through.
### Cuts and preparation
- Supreme: breast with skin and the first wing bone attached (French-trimmed). Fillet/breast, tenderloin (inner fillet), Maryland (whole leg: thigh + drumstick), drumstick, thigh (bone in or fillet), wing (drumette and wingette), frame/carcass (for stock).
- Jointing a chicken into 4 (2 breasts, 2 legs), 8 (2 drumsticks, 2 thighs, 2 wings, 2 breasts) or 10 pieces (breasts halved).
- Spatchcock (butterfly): remove the backbone with poultry shears and flatten — cooks quickly and evenly on the grill.
- Trussing: I tuck the wing tips under and cross the legs. I tie the legs and tail with butcher's string and loop it around the body. The bird keeps a compact shape, cooks evenly, holds the stuffing in and carves neatly.
- Ballotine: boned leg (or bird) stuffed, rolled and tied, roasted or braised — served hot. Galantine: boned bird stuffed, rolled, poached, served cold. Crown: whole breast on the bone (legs removed). Paupiette: thin escalope rolled around a filling.
- Stuffing/farce: cook stuffing separately or ensure the centre reaches 75°C; stuff just before cooking.
- Cooking methods: roasting (whole birds — rest 10–15 min), grilling/char-grilling (breast, thigh, spatchcock), pan-frying, deep frying (crumbed/battered — southern fried, karaage, schnitzel), poaching (breast, whole chicken — in stock), steaming, braising/stewing (coq au vin, cacciatore, fricassée, curries), stir-frying, sous vide, smoking, confit (below).
- Classic dishes: roast chicken, chicken Kiev (garlic butter, crumbed, fried), cordon bleu (ham and cheese), parmigiana, coq au vin (red wine, lardons, mushrooms), chasseur, cacciatore, fricassée (white stew), butter chicken, satay, green curry, Peking duck, duck à l'orange, roast turkey with cranberry.
### Poultry assessment answers
- Crispy duck skin: score the skin without cutting the meat, dry uncovered in the fridge (salt), start skin-side down in a cold or medium pan to render the fat slowly, pour off fat, finish in the oven; whole duck: scald, dry overnight, roast on a rack.
- Confit: cure legs in salt, garlic and thyme 12–24 h, rinse, dry, cover in duck fat, cook slowly (oven about 120–140°C, 2–3 h) until tender; store covered in the fat ≤5°C, labelled; crisp the skin before service; reheat thoroughly. Risk: low-oxygen fat storage (C. botulinum) — keep cold, use-by. Duck legs aren't poached: water doesn't render fat or crisp skin and the meat goes stringy; fat keeps it moist and rich.
- Offal (livers, hearts, gizzards): high moisture, spoils fast — ≤5°C on the bottom shelf, use in 1–2 days or freeze; cook chicken livers through (Campylobacter).
- Pale roast chicken skin: oven too cool, wet skin, no fat on the skin, bird covered or crowded (steams). Fix: raise the heat or finish under the salamander, brush with butter; next time dry the skin.
- 8 pieces: cut the skin between leg and body, pop the hip joint, remove the legs; split each at the knee (thigh, drumstick); remove the wings; cut out the backbone; split the breast in two. Sharp boning knife, yellow board, stable surface, sanitise after.
- Juicy breast: brine, even thickness, cook to the safe core then rest (carry-over), baste, skin on; don't overcook.
- Raw poultry dripped in the cool room: throw out any ready-to-eat food it touched, move the poultry to a covered tray on the bottom shelf, clean and sanitise shelves and floor, wash hands, record and report.
- Portions: weigh to the standard recipe (e.g. 180–220 g breast, half or quarter bird).
- Reheating poultry dishes: I reheat once only, fast, in the oven, on the stove or in the microwave, never in a bain-marie. I heat it to 75°C in the centre (Code: ≥60°C within 2 hours). I stir and probe it. I serve it at once or hold it ≥60°C. I throw out what is left.

## Meat
### Structure and tenderness
- Muscle fibres held together by connective tissue: collagen (softens to gelatine with long, slow, moist cooking) and elastin (yellow "silverskin" — does not break down; trim it off).
- Tender cuts come from muscles that do little work: loin, fillet, rib. I cook them fast with dry heat: grill, pan-fry, roast. Tough cuts come from hard-working muscles: shoulder, shin, neck, brisket, cheek, shank. I cook them slowly with moist heat: braise, stew, pot roast, sous vide. Dry heat cooks without liquid and browns the surface. Moist heat uses liquid or steam below boiling and turns collagen into gelatine.
- Marbling: intramuscular fat that adds flavour and juiciness (graded by MSA — Meat Standards Australia, and marble scores e.g. Wagyu).
- Tenderising: ageing (dry ageing in controlled cool room for weeks — concentrated flavour, weight loss; wet ageing in vacuum bags), mechanical (pounding/batting out escalopes, mincing, scoring, needling), marinating (acids: wine, vinegar, citrus, yoghurt; enzymes: papain from papaya, bromelain from pineapple, actinidin from kiwifruit), cutting across the grain, slow cooking.
- Carry-over cooking: the core keeps rising about 3–5°C after the meat leaves the heat. So I take it off a few degrees early. Resting (5–10 min for steaks, 15–20 min for roasts) lets the juices settle and the fibres relax. The meat is juicier and loses less juice when cut.
- Doneness by touch/temperature (core): rare 50–52°C (red centre), medium-rare 55–57°C, medium 60–63°C (pink), medium-well 65–68°C, well done 70°C+ (no pink). Mince, sausages, rolled/stuffed meats: 75°C. Pork: cook until just cooked (around 70°C, juices clear) — modern restaurants may serve some pork cuts slightly pink following workplace policy.

### Veal
Young calf, pale and lean: schnitzel/escalopes (leg), cutlets (loin), osso buco (braised shank with gremolata), saltimbocca, scaloppine, vitello tonnato.
### Lamb
- Classes: lamb (under 12 months/no permanent incisor teeth), hogget (one to two permanent incisors), mutton (older, stronger flavour).
- Cuts: rack (French-trimmed; cutlets), loin (chops, backstrap — lean, quick), leg (roast, butterflied, steaks), shoulder (slow roast, braise, curries), shank (braise), neck (stew), breast/flap (slow cook), mince.
- Dishes: roast leg, herb-crusted rack, slow-roast shoulder, navarin, Irish stew, moussaka, shepherd's pie, tagine, kofta, rogan josh, braised shanks.
### Pork
- Cuts: loin (roast, cutlets, chops, rack), fillet/tenderloin, belly (roast for crackling, braise, cure for bacon/pancetta), leg (roast, ham), shoulder/forequarter (scotch fillet/collar butt, blade — slow roast, pulled pork, char siu), spare ribs, hock and trotters, mince.
- Crackling: score the rind, dry well (refrigerate uncovered), rub with oil and salt, start in a hot oven (220–240°C) then reduce.
### Processed and cured meats
Sausages, bacon, ham, prosciutto, pancetta, salami, chorizo, corned beef, pastrami, terrines and pâtés.
### Mince
Highest risk, because surface bacteria are mixed all through it. I use it within 1–2 days and cook it to 75°C. Dishes: burgers, meatballs, rissoles, Bolognese, lasagne, cottage pie, kofta.
### Meat assessment answers
- Spoilage — reject or discard: slimy or sticky surface, sour or off smell, grey-green colour, swollen or leaking vacuum packs, above 5°C on delivery, past use-by.
- Pork belly: score the skin, dry uncovered overnight, rub with salt, slow-roast about 150–160°C for 2–3 h to render the fat, then blast at 220–240°C to crackle (or braise, press, chill, portion and crisp).
- Beef cheek braise: trim sinew, season, sear hard, remove; sweat mirepoix, add tomato paste, deglaze with red wine and reduce; return cheeks, add brown stock two-thirds up and a bouquet garni; cover, braise ~150°C for 3–4 h until fork-tender; strain, reduce the sauce to nappe, season.
- Slice against the grain: cuts the muscle fibres short, so the meat is tender to chew.
- Tough, dry steak — three common causes: it was overcooked (no probe, left too long); it was not rested, or cut with the grain; it was the wrong cut (a tough lean cut cooked fast). Fix: I probe, rest, slice against the grain and match the cut to the method.
- Searing and the Maillard reaction: I put a dry surface in a very hot pan with a little oil, above about 140°C. Proteins and sugars brown. This gives crust, colour and deep flavour. It does not "seal in" juices.
- Roasting cuts: rib/scotch fillet, striploin, rump, topside, lamb leg, rack and shoulder, pork loin, leg and belly, whole birds. Braising cuts: chuck, blade, brisket, shin, cheek, oxtail, short ribs, lamb shank, shoulder and neck, pork shoulder and hock.
- Less shrinkage and yield loss: moderate cooking temperatures, don't overcook (probe), rest before slicing, accurate trimming and portioning with scales, carve correctly, use trimmings and juices.
- Receiving and storage records: delivery temperature, supplier, use-by and batch, pack condition, rejections, fridge temperature log, labels and rotation.

## Preparation techniques for meat and poultry
Trimming fat, sinew and silverskin, boning out, French-trimming racks (exposing the bone), butterflying, batting out (escalopes, schnitzel), portioning to standard weights (e.g. 200–250 g steak), dicing for stews (2–3 cm), tying joints, larding (fat strips inside lean meat) and barding (wrapping in fat or bacon), marinating, crumbing (pané), stuffing, mincing, brining, dry rubs.

## Accompaniments and sauces (classic pairings)
Roast beef — Yorkshire pudding, horseradish, red wine jus; steak — Béarnaise, pepper or Diane sauce; lamb — mint, rosemary jus; pork — apple sauce; chicken — gravy; turkey — cranberry; duck — orange or cherry; osso buco — gremolata.

## Food safety for meat and poultry
I receive and store meat ≤5°C. Vacuum packs must be intact, with no swelling or off smell. It goes on the bottom shelf, covered, raw apart from RTE. Red board for meat, yellow for poultry. I cook to safe core temperatures, cool by the 2h/4h rule and label with date and use-by.
