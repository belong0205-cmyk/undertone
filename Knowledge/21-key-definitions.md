# Key definitions — quick answers to "Define / Explain / List" questions (SIT30821)

One-line definitions with the numbers assessors expect. Details are in the unit notes.

## Sauces, emulsions and thickening
- Emulsion: two liquids that normally don't mix (fat and water) held together as tiny droplets. Temporary: separates on standing (vinaigrette, 3 oil : 1 vinegar). Semi-permanent: stays mixed for a while, helped by mustard. Permanent: stabilised by an emulsifier (mayonnaise; warm emulsions hollandaise, beurre blanc).
- Emulsifier: an ingredient that holds an emulsion together, e.g. lecithin in egg yolk, mustard.
- Roux: equal weights of fat (usually butter) and flour cooked together to thicken sauces. White roux (1–2 min, no colour: béchamel), blond roux (sandy, light straw colour: velouté), brown roux (nut brown: espagnole; thickens less).
- Mother (leading) sauces — list: béchamel (milk + white roux), velouté (white stock + blond roux), espagnole (brown stock + brown roux), tomato sauce, hollandaise (egg yolks + clarified butter). Mayonnaise is the cold emulsion sauce.
- Derivative (small) sauce: made from a mother sauce plus flavourings, e.g. Mornay (béchamel + cheese), Béarnaise (hollandaise + tarragon), Bordelaise (demi-glace + red wine).
- Demi-glace: espagnole + brown stock reduced by half.
- Liaison: egg yolks + cream used to thicken and enrich at the end; never boil after adding.
- Beurre manié: raw butter and flour kneaded together, whisked in at the end to thicken.
- Slurry: cornflour or arrowroot mixed with cold liquid, whisked into simmering liquid.
- Reduction: simmering a liquid so water evaporates, thickening it and concentrating flavour.
- Nappe: a sauce thick enough to coat the back of a spoon.
- Monter au beurre: whisking cold butter into a sauce at the end for shine and richness.
- Clarified butter: butter with water and milk solids removed; higher smoke point; used for hollandaise and frying.
- Jus: natural meat juices from roasting, often lightly reduced; jus lié is lightly thickened.

## Cooking terms and methods
- Mise en place: everything prepared and in its place before service.
- Blanch and refresh: cook briefly in boiling water, then plunge into iced water to stop cooking and keep colour.
- Deglaze: dissolve the browned residue in a pan with liquid (wine, stock) to make a sauce.
- Sweat: cook gently in a little fat, covered or uncovered, without colour.
- Sauté: cook small pieces quickly in a little hot fat, tossing.
- Braise vs stew: braise = large pieces seared, then cooked slowly with a small amount of liquid; stew = small pieces fully covered in liquid; both are slow, moist methods for tougher cuts.
- Poach: cook gently in liquid at about 70–85°C (no bubbles). Simmer: about 85–95°C with small bubbles. Boil: 100°C.
- Sous vide: vacuum-sealed food cooked in a precisely controlled water bath at a low temperature.
- En papillote: food baked in a sealed paper or foil parcel so it steams in its own juices.
- Al dente: pasta or vegetables cooked until firm to the bite.
- Au gratin: topped with cheese or breadcrumbs and browned under a salamander or in the oven.
- Dredge: coat food lightly in flour (or sugar), then shake off the excess.
- Macerate: soak fruit in sugar, liqueur or juice to soften it and add flavour.
- Smorgasbord: a Scandinavian-style buffet of many cold and hot dishes.
- Maillard reaction: browning of proteins and sugars with dry heat above about 140°C (flavour and colour).
- Caramelisation: browning of sugar, from about 160°C.
- Gelatinisation: starch granules absorb liquid and swell when heated, thickening it (sauces, rice).
- Coagulation: proteins setting with heat or acid (eggs set at about 62–70°C).
- Carry-over cooking: food keeps cooking from its own heat after it leaves the heat; rest meat.
- Bain-marie: a water bath for gentle cooking or for keeping food hot (≥60°C), not for reheating.

## Pastry and desserts
- Tempering eggs: slowly whisking hot liquid into eggs to raise their temperature without scrambling them (custards).
- Crème anglaise vs crème pâtissière: anglaise is a pouring custard (milk/cream, yolks, sugar) cooked to 82–84°C, never boiled; pâtissière is a thick pastry cream with flour/cornflour and must be boiled 1–2 minutes.
- Sabayon: egg yolks and sugar (and wine) whisked over heat to a light foam.
- Chantilly cream: cream whipped with sugar and vanilla.
- Gluten: the elastic network formed by wheat proteins (glutenin and gliadin) when flour is mixed with water and kneaded.
- Proving: letting a yeast dough rise; knock back to remove large gas pockets.
- Lamination: folding butter into dough in many thin layers (puff pastry, croissants); steam lifts the layers.
- Blind baking: baking a pastry case lined with paper and baking weights before filling, to stop a soggy base.
- Bloom (chocolate): grey/white streaks from fat (poor tempering, heat) or sugar (moisture).
- Seized chocolate: thick and grainy because a little water got in.

## Food safety, WHS and kitchen management
- Temperature danger zone: 5–60°C, where bacteria grow fastest.
- Potentially hazardous food: food that must be kept at 5°C or below or 60°C or above to stay safe (meat, dairy, eggs, cooked rice, seafood) (3.2.2 cl 1).
- Cross-contamination: moving bacteria or allergens from one food, surface or person to another.
- HACCP: Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points — a food safety system with 7 principles that controls hazards at critical points (receiving, cooking, cooling, storage).
- Critical control point (CCP): a step where control is essential to keep food safe, e.g. cooking to 75°C.
- 2-hour/4-hour rule: ready-to-eat potentially hazardous food out of temperature control under 2 h → use or refrigerate; 2–4 h → use now; over 4 h → throw out (Safe Food Australia App. 2).
- Cleaning vs sanitising: cleaning removes food, dirt and grease; sanitising then uses heat (77°C water for 30 s), chemicals or both to reduce micro-organisms to a safe level, not to zero (3.2.2 cl 20). Always clean first.
- FIFO: first in, first out stock rotation. FEFO: first expired, first out.
- Par level: the stock level needed between deliveries.
- Standard recipe: the tested recipe with exact quantities, method, yield, portion size and cost, used every time for consistency.
- Yield %: usable weight ÷ purchased weight × 100. Food cost %: food cost ÷ selling price (ex-GST) × 100.
- Hierarchy of control — list: elimination, substitution, isolation, engineering controls, administrative controls, PPE.
- SDS: Safety Data Sheet — the supplier's safety information for a chemical.
- PPE: personal protective equipment (gloves, non-slip shoes, apron, goggles, oven mitts).
